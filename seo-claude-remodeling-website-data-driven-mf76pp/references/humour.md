# Humour — My Dream Home Remodeling

> Home renovation is a five-figure decision homeowners are usually a little
> anxious about. The humour here is dry and confident, not slapstick — it earns
> trust by being self-aware about the process, never at the customer's expense.
> This is the optional half of the voice; `voice.md` is the non-negotiable half.

## The north-star

This isn't a joke-a-minute blog. It's a contractor who's seen enough bathrooms
mid-demo to find the process a little funny, and isn't afraid to say so — while
still being the person you trust with a $25,000 remodel. Dry, understated,
earned. Never goofy.

## The opening rule

A post doesn't need a joke in the first sentence. It needs to sound like a
person, not a brochure. One dry, self-aware line somewhere in the first
paragraph is enough — don't force a punchline before the reader knows what
the page is even about.

## Rules for humour

### 1. Punch at the process, never at the reader.

The renovation process is genuinely funny in retrospect — the decision fatigue
of picking tile grout, the "just one more thing" moment during demo, the fact
that everyone underestimates how loud a jackhammer is indoors. That's fair
game. The homeowner's taste, budget, or house is never the joke.

- "Grout color is the decision that breaks people. We've seen homeowners agonize
  over 'bone' versus 'oyster white' longer than they spent picking their house."
- "Demo day is loud. Not 'close the door' loud — 'text your neighbors first' loud."

### 2. Self-deprecation about the trade, not the customer.

- "We've measured a bathroom twice and still had to send someone back for a
  third look. Measure twice, cut once — allegedly."
- "Every contractor says 'two to three weeks.' We say it because it's usually
  true, which is apparently the unusual part."

### 3. Dry asides, used sparingly.

A short parenthetical that reminds the reader a person wrote this — not a
running bit.

- "(Yes, the waterproofing membrane is boring. It's also the part that
  determines whether you're doing this again in five years.)"
- "(We know. Everyone says 'licensed and insured.' Ask to see it anyway.)"

### 4. The sign-off should land, not just close.

Not:

> "Contact us today for your free estimate!"

But:

> "Most contractors will tell you what you want to hear. We'll tell you what
> your bathroom actually needs — then give you a number in writing."

## Anti-patterns — never do these

- **Exclamation marks.** Confidence reads better deadpan.
- **Emojis.** Never.
- **Jokes about the homeowner's budget, taste, or existing house.** The old
  bathroom being ugly is fine to say plainly; mocking the person who lived
  with it isn't.
- **Forced quirk.** If a line isn't genuinely dry-funny, cut it — a flat
  paragraph beats a bad joke.
- **Fabricated personal anecdotes.** Don't invent a story about Yojainier's
  life to make a joke land. If there's no real detail to hang it on, keep the
  humour about the trade, not the person.
- **Overuse.** One or two moments per page, not one per paragraph. This is a
  remodeling company, not a comedy blog.

- A brochure ✗
